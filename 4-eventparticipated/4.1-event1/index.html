Event 1

Summary Report: “From First Cloud AI Journey to AWS Partner” – FCAJ Meetup

Event Objectives

  • Introduce the First Cloud AI Journey (FCAJ) Program and the career paths it opens up after graduation
  • Share real, first-hand stories of working as a Solutions Architect, Data Analytics Engineer, and DevOps Engineer
  • Help students build the right mindset and skill set before entering the workforce
  • Connect the AWS community (Student Builder Group, Community Builder, AWS Partners) with students and young professionals

Speakers

  • Danh Hoang Hieu Nghi – AI Engineer, AWS Community Builder, AWS Student Builder Group Leader
  • Mr. Dat Pham – Data Analytics Engineer
  • Mr. Cuong Nguyen – Process Engineer
  • Trong H. Truong – DevOps Engineer, Endava Vietnam
  • Kien & Tho – Solutions Architects

Key Highlights

From First Cloud AI Journey Program to AWS Partner

  • FCAJ Program (formerly First Cloud Journey Program): equips students with cloud/AI foundations as a starting point, not an end point — “Getting the job is just a beginning.”
  • Career paths after FCAJ: Solutions Architect, Head of Solutions Architect, DevOps Engineer, Platform Engineer, Software Engineer — the message was to “write your own history.”
  • AWS Student Builder Group Program (formerly AWS Cloud Clubs Program): a community for students to keep building and learning together (builder.aws.com/community/student-builder-groups).
  • AWS Community Builder Program: a path for individuals to deepen technical expertise and give back to the community.
  • AWS Partners: examples of local AWS partners recognized for their achievements, including an “AWS Partner of the Year – Vietnam 2026” award, showing where a cloud career can eventually lead.

The real work of a Data Analytics Engineer

  • The day-to-day work of a Data Analytics Engineer differs a lot depending on the industry, business model, and department it supports.
  • Kamereo case study: building daily/weekly/monthly/quarterly reports, designing dashboards to track trends and catch anomalies, analyzing business/operational metrics to find root causes, and coordinating across departments to solve real problems.
  • Colgate-Palmolive case study: working with factory machinery and IoT data, looking for opportunities to optimize production costs and improve operational efficiency, and supporting digital transformation initiatives.
  • Essential skills: critical thinking, communication, storytelling with data, and problem-solving — turning raw numbers into a story that drives action.
  • Real example shared: when preparing a report, going beyond just handing numbers to a manager — digging into why GMV fluctuated and proposing concrete improvements.

What a DevOps Engineer really does

  • Common misconception vs. reality: job descriptions often reduce DevOps to “the person who writes CI/CD pipelines,” “the Docker/Kubernetes person,” or “the one who fixes production at midnight” — the real job is much broader than any single tool.
  • What DevOps really does in real life: owning the whole path from build → test → deploy → monitor, and taking responsibility for the systems in production, not just the pipeline.
  • What to learn first: fundamentals before tools — Linux, networking basics, a programming language (Python/Go), Git and CI/CD, containers, and understanding how applications actually run (build, test, deploy, logs, configuration, environment variables) — then reinforce it by building and breaking small projects.
  • Using AI tools (ChatGPT, Claude, DeepSeek) as a way to leverage skills, not a replacement for understanding.
  • Lessons learned the hard way: copying commands is not the same as understanding them; learn to identify the real owner of a problem; ask “why” before “how”; communication is part of the job; DevOps is not about being a hero.
  • What makes a good DevOps engineer: staying curious, thinking in systems rather than isolated tasks, remembering that tools change but fundamentals stay, making things clear for the team, and automating boring, repetitive work.

A closer look at the Solutions Architect role

  • Contrasted what people think a Solutions Architect does with what the role actually involves day to day — from designing solutions to communicating trade-offs with customers and internal teams.
  • Walked through the skills and mindset needed to grow into and succeed in the role.
  • Shared a practical view of what a career path toward becoming a Solutions Architect can look like.

Key Takeaways

Career mindset

  • A first job or a certificate is only a starting point — real growth comes from what you build afterward.
  • Different companies and industries can shape the same job title (e.g., Data Analytics Engineer) into very different day-to-day work.
  • Community programs (Student Builder Group, Community Builder) are a practical way to keep learning and networking after a program like FCAJ ends.

Technical foundations

  • Across every role discussed — Data Analytics, DevOps, Solutions Architecture — strong fundamentals (data, systems, networking, cloud) matter more than mastering any single tool.
  • Tools and platforms change quickly; the underlying fundamentals stay relevant much longer.
  • AI tools are most valuable as an aid to thinking and productivity, not a substitute for understanding.

Soft skills

  • Communication and storytelling turn technical work into something the business can act on.
  • Asking “why” before “how” and identifying the real owner of a problem lead to better outcomes than jumping straight to execution.
  • Cross-department collaboration is a recurring part of real technical work, not an occasional exception.

Applying to Work

  • Look beyond the job title: research how the same role differs across companies/industries before choosing a direction.
  • Build fundamentals first: prioritize Linux, networking, a core programming language, Git/CI-CD, and containers over chasing every new tool.
  • Practice storytelling with data: when presenting results, explain the “why” behind the numbers, not just the numbers themselves.
  • Use AI tools deliberately: use them to speed up work and learning, while still making sure I understand what the tool produced.
  • Join the community: consider engaging with the AWS Student Builder Group / Community Builder programs to keep learning and networking beyond this event.

Event Experience

Attending the “From First Cloud AI Journey to AWS Partner” FCAJ Meetup gave me a much clearer, more grounded picture of what different cloud and data careers actually look like day to day. Key experiences included:

Learning from professionals across different roles

  • Hearing directly from a Data Analytics Engineer, a Process Engineer, a DevOps Engineer, and Solutions Architects gave me several honest, first-hand perspectives instead of a single generic view of “working in tech.”
  • The contrast between how the same role plays out at different companies (e.g., Kamereo vs. Colgate-Palmolive) made it clear that the industry and business context shape the job as much as the title does.

Rethinking common misconceptions

  • The DevOps session in particular reshaped how I think about the role — it’s not “the Docker person” or “the one who fixes things at midnight,” but someone who understands and takes responsibility for the whole system.
  • I came away with a much better sense of what to prioritize when learning: fundamentals first, tools second.

Practical, actionable advice

  • The “what to learn first” guidance (Linux, networking, a programming language, Git/CI-CD, containers, small hands-on projects) gave me a concrete starting checklist rather than vague encouragement.
  • The reminder to ask “why” before “how,” and to identify who really owns a problem, is something I can apply immediately in my own project work.

Community and career paths

  • Learning about the AWS Student Builder Group and Community Builder programs showed me practical ways to keep growing after this internship/program ends.
  • Seeing the range of paths that came out of the FCAJ program — Solutions Architect, DevOps Engineer, Platform Engineer, Software Engineer — was a good reminder that there isn’t one “correct” path to follow.

Overall, this event helped me move from an abstract idea of “working in cloud/data” to a much more concrete understanding of what different roles actually require, and gave me practical guidance on what to focus on next in my own learning.
